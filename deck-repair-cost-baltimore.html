Why there's no ledger-board line item here

The ledger board — the piece that bolts your deck to the house — is the most failure-prone component on a deck, and it is also the one repair for which no published cost figure exists. We're not going to invent one.

In practice, ledger work is priced from the bands above after an inspection: the flashing running the length of the connection, the joists that have to come off and go back, and whatever rot is found in the rim joist and the framing around it. Whether the house is brick-veneered matters too, because the code prohibits attaching a ledger to brick or stone veneer — it isn't structural — so a deck found hanging on veneer is a different conversation entirely. What actually drives a Baltimore-area quote up or down?

National tables can't see your deck. These six variables are what we're actually looking at when we price one here:

  • Deck height and access. A deck two steps off the ground is a different job from one two storeys up over a walkout in Ellicott City's valley lots, and a rowhome roof deck in Canton is different again, because every board and every bag of debris goes up and down through the house.
  • How much framing is affected. This is the biggest single variable. Surface damage is priced per board; rot that has reached joists, posts or the ledger moves you into the structural bands above.
  • Stairs. Stringers, treads, handrails and footings each carry their own labor, and stair stringers resting on soil or block rather than a proper footing usually mean more work than the homeowner expected.
  • Railing material. Look again at that material table — the difference between wood and glass is roughly five times per linear foot, and that choice alone can reshape a quote.
  • Finish choice. Clean-and-seal, semi-transparent stain, solid stain and full refinishing are different amounts of prep and different products. Sealers generally need redoing every 12–18 months, semi-transparent stains last 12–18 months on horizontal surfaces, and solid colour holds 1–3 years horizontally.
  • Whether a permit is triggered. Rules change at every county line. Baltimore County requires a permit above 16 inches of height or 120 square feet; Howard County treats board-only replacement as ordinary repair; Anne Arundel always requires one for an attached deck; Baltimore City has no exemption at all. Staining, sealing and power washing aren't addressed in any county's permit materials. See the Baltimore County permit guide.

Where deck money gets wasted

Three patterns, over and over: refinishing a deck whose framing is failing, so the money is gone in two years; replacing a whole deck when the frame was sound and only the surface and railings were finished; and paying for repeated small fixes at a ledger or a rooftop membrane that needed one real repair. We serve Lutherville-Timonium, Severna Park, Federal Hill and the rest of the metro with the same rule: we tell you which of those you're looking at before you spend anything.

Common Questions

Deck Repair Cost FAQs

Why can't you just publish Baltimore deck repair prices?

Because no Maryland or Baltimore-specific published cost data exists for deck repair, and we won't dress up a national average as a local price. What we can do is show you every published national figure, name the source, and then explain honestly which local variables move your number — deck height, access, how much framing is affected, stairs, railing material, finish choice and whether a permit is triggered. Then we come out, look at the deck, and put a real number in writing at no charge.

What is the cheapest deck repair, and what is the most expensive?

Nationally, Bob Vila puts the smallest items at the bottom of the scale: popped screws at $100–$200 and missing nails at $100–$200, with cracks and holes at $150–$500 each. At the top of the same national list are sagging at $750–$10,000, termite damage at $500–$10,000 and rot at $300–$5,000, because all three mean framing work rather than surface work. Baltimore-area pricing varies with access, deck height and framing condition — the gap between a $200 visit and a five-figure structural project is almost always about what's happening below the boards.

How much does it cost to replace deck boards?

Nationally, Bob Vila puts a board replacement project at $500–$4,000 and resurfacing at $15–$50 per square foot; Fixr puts board replacement at $15–$55 per square foot, and floor board repair runs $10–$35 per square foot. Material choice moves it: nationally, materials alone run $3–$45 per square foot, with cedar at $25–$55 and composite at $12–$45 installed. Baltimore-area pricing varies with access, deck height and framing condition — matching an existing board profile and blending the repair so it doesn't look patched is usually what separates a good board job from a cheap one.

Do you charge for an estimate or an inspection?

No. Estimates are free and in writing, and that includes the walk-around where we check the ledger connection, framing, posts, footings, railings and stairs rather than only the thing you called about. If the honest answer is that your deck is past saving, you'll get that for free too — we'd rather tell you than sell you a repair that buys eighteen months.

Is repairing a deck cheaper than replacing it?

Almost always, when the framing is sound. If joists, beams, posts and the ledger are solid and the damage is limited to decking, railings or finish, repair costs a fraction of replacement. Replacement starts to make sense when framing rot is widespread, when the structure can't be brought up to current code, or when repair costs approach half the price of a new deck. That's a judgment call we make on site and explain before any work starts.
