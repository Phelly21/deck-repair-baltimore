Rooftop deck work in Federal Hill runs on a different clock than anywhere else we work, because Federal Hill is a CHAP historic district and CHAP approval has to come before the building permit. Get that order wrong and the project restarts. The Deck Repair Wizard repairs rowhome rooftop decks here — membranes, flashing, rotted framing over the roof, railings — and we sequence the approvals properly instead of discovering them halfway through.

The approval sequence, in the order it actually happens

This is the part almost nobody publishes, and it is the most valuable thing on this page:

  1. Confirm the address is inside the district. Historic district boundaries do not follow neighborhood nicknames. This is step zero, before any drawing gets made.
  2. CHAP approval first. In a CHAP historic district, approval is required before the building permit. Filing for the permit first is the single most common and most expensive mistake on a Federal Hill roof deck.
  3. Then the building permit, with construction documents signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland — a requirement for rooftop structures across the city.
  4. Written notification to adjoining property owners, with evidence of that notice submitted alongside the construction documents. On a Federal Hill block of shared party walls, that is a real step with real neighbors.
  5. City inspections — footing and final.

What CHAP is actually weighing. Rooftop decks are not banned in the historic district, but they are often disallowed or strictly limited, and they are reviewed for street visibility. No deck may be visible from the front of the structure. A deck visible from the rear alley may be approvable. Stair penthouses — "doghouses" — are discouraged, with internal stair or hatch access preferred. And raw, unpainted pressure-treated lumber is generally not approved.

Federal Hill's rowhomes and the decks added to them

Median home values in Federal Hill run around $340,000, and the neighborhood's rowhome stock has been through a long renovation cycle. The rooftop decks that came out of that cycle were largely installed during the 2000s and 2010s, which means the typical Federal Hill roof deck is now 10 to 20-plus years old — the exact window in which membranes, flashing and railings stop performing.

What we find here is consistent. The waterproofing membrane under the deck is the number one failure: blistering, alligatoring, and cracking driven by ultraviolet exposure and thermal movement. The region gives it plenty to work with — roughly 45 inches of rain a year (the sum of the National Weather Service's 1991–2020 monthly normals at BWI), humidity near 75% from August through October, a freeze-free season of more than 230 days, and winter temperatures crossing back and forth over freezing repeatedly. Second most common is a leaking penetration: every post base and curb passing through the deck has to be flashed, and an unflashed one leaks years before the membrane gives out. Third is the wrong system altogether — a standard roofing membrane installed where a pedestrian deck waterproofing system belongs will crack within a couple of seasons under foot traffic and furniture.

Below the boards, the sleepers and framing sitting on a damp roof surface rot from the bottom up while the walking surface still looks fine. And the railings — guards over 30 inches above the walking surface must be at least 36 inches high on a home, with no opening a 4-inch sphere can pass through — loosen quietly over the same fifteen years.

Baltimore City permit rules that apply on top of CHAP

Historic district review does not replace the building code — it stacks on top of it. Two things Federal Hill homeowners routinely underestimate:

  • Baltimore City has no deck exemption at all. Decks are absent from the city's list of work exempt from permit. There is no square-footage and no height threshold, unlike Baltimore County's 16-inch or 120-square-foot trigger. The city carries general ordinary-repairs language, but that is a narrow lane rather than a size exemption.
  • The rooftop structures rules are numeric and strict. No rooftop deck may be raised more than 2 feet above the plane midway between the lowest and highest points of the roof surface. Setbacks: 8 feet from the face of the building wall on a flat roof, 6 feet behind the ridge on a gabled or mansard roof. No side or rear setback is required.

Those numbers matter on repairs, not only on new construction — rebuilding a deck slightly higher, or pushing it forward to gain usable area, can take a deck that was compliant and make it non-compliant. Our Baltimore City rooftop deck permit and CHAP guide covers the whole process in detail.

Why the structure itself is different here

A Baltimore rowhome rooftop deck typically spans party wall to party wall on shared masonry rather than hanging from a wood ledger bolted to a rim joist. Every prescriptive back-yard deck detail in the code assumes the ledger arrangement, which is why the sealed drawings are mandatory for rooftop structures — and why a contractor who prices a Federal Hill roof deck like a suburban deck is working from the wrong model entirely. The load path runs into masonry you share with your neighbor. That is a structural conversation, not a decking conversation.

Federal Hill Questions

Rooftop Deck FAQs for Federal Hill Homeowners

Do I need CHAP approval before a building permit in Federal Hill?

Yes — that order matters and it is where most Federal Hill projects go wrong. Federal Hill is a CHAP historic district, and CHAP approval is required before the building permit, not alongside it and not after. Homeowners who apply for the permit first generally end up restarting. The practical sequence is: confirm your address is inside the district, prepare drawings that address what CHAP reviews, obtain CHAP approval, then file for the building permit with construction documents signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland. We will not quote a Federal Hill rooftop project as though the approval step does not exist.

Are rooftop decks allowed in the Federal Hill historic district?

They are not banned, but they are frequently disallowed or strictly limited, and they are reviewed for street visibility. The governing principle is that no deck may be visible from the front of the structure. A deck visible from the rear alley may be approvable. Every Federal Hill block is different in how it reads from the street, and rooftop projects here live or die on that sightline question — which is why the honest first step is a look at the specific house rather than a general answer.

Can I put a stair penthouse on my Federal Hill rowhome?

Rooftop stair penthouses — the enclosures commonly called doghouses — are discouraged in CHAP historic districts. Internal stair or hatch access is the preferred approach. This comes up constantly on repair projects, because a homeowner who wants easier roof access often assumes a penthouse is the fix. In a district like Federal Hill it is generally the part of the plan most likely to draw objection, and rethinking access early saves a great deal of time.

Can I use pressure-treated lumber on a Federal Hill roof deck?

Raw, unpainted pressure-treated lumber is generally not approved in CHAP historic districts. That does not mean the material is off the table, but it does mean the finish and appearance of what shows has to be addressed in the submission rather than treated as an afterthought. On a repair this matters when boards or railing components are being replaced: matching what is already there is usually the smoothest path, and changing the visible material is a decision to make before the application, not during the job.

How high and how far back does Baltimore City allow a rooftop deck?

The city's rooftop structures rules apply in Federal Hill on top of the CHAP review. No rooftop deck may be raised more than 2 feet above the plane midway between the lowest and highest points of the roof surface. On a flat roof the deck must sit 8 feet back from the face of the building wall; on a gabled or mansard roof it must be 6 feet behind the ridge. No side or rear setback is required. Adjoining property owners must be notified in writing, with evidence of that notice submitted alongside the construction documents, and those documents must be signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland.
