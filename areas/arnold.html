If your deck in Arnold goes green every summer and the finish never seems to last, the cause is not the stain you bought — it is the shade. Arnold sits on the Broadneck Peninsula between the Severn and the Magothy under heavy tree cover, with 20.18% of the area made up of water. Deep shade plus waterfront humidity is the single fastest way to destroy a deck finish and start rot underneath it, and it is the reason most of our Arnold work is stripping, repairing and refinishing rather than rebuilding.

Shade and humidity: why Arnold decks fail the way they do

Arnold runs a median household income around $145,416 and a median home value near $586,387, on a peninsula with 12 marinas and a documented undisturbed forest natural area at Chase Creek Woods. That canopy is the reason people move here — and it is exactly what a deck finish cannot survive.

Here is the mechanism. The area gets roughly 45 inches of rain a year, the sum of the National Weather Service's 1991–2020 monthly normals at BWI. Humidity sits near 75% from August through October, and the freeze-free season runs over 230 days, so there is a long window each year when wood stays damp. Leaf and pine-needle debris blocks sunlight and compounds algae and mildew growth. Horizontal deck boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it — which is why the walking surface always fails first, and why a shaded Arnold deck can be visibly further gone than a sunny deck of the same age five miles inland.

Then winter: temperatures swing above and below freezing repeatedly, so water that has soaked into open joints, board ends and fastener holes expands and pries them wider every season. Green film is a wet-shade problem and black streaking is mildew. Both are slip hazards, and both mean the surface has been holding moisture long enough for the framing underneath to be worth checking.

Finish schedules that actually match this environment

Manufacturer intervals assume average exposure, and Arnold is not average. As a working baseline: water-repellent sealers generally need reapplication every 12 to 18 months and sometimes every 6 to 12 under sun and traffic; semi-transparent and transparent stains run 12 to 18 months on horizontal surfaces; solid-color finishes last one to three years on horizontals versus three to seven on vertical faces. The simplest test is the spring water-bead check — splash water on the boards, and if it soaks in rather than beading, the finish is spent and the wood is absorbing every rain.

One caution specific to this kind of deck: power washing is the right first step but only at controlled pressure. Too much pressure permanently furs the wood fibers, and furred wood holds more water and more dirt than it did before. The correct sequence is clean or strip, dry properly, sand where needed, then seal — because finish applied to damp wood fails within a season. Anne Arundel County permit rules for Arnold

Anne Arundel is the only county in this metro that separates attached decks from detached ones, and the difference is not a technicality:

  • An attached deck always requires a permit — any size, any height, no exceptions for being small or low.
  • A detached deck is exempt only if it is under 200 square feet and under 3 feet high and outside the Critical Area. All three, together.
  • Board-only replacement is exempt as ordinary repair, so re-decking sound framing needs no permit.
  • Inspections run footing, framing and final, and the county works to the 2021 International Residential Code.
  • Staining, sealing and power washing are not addressed in the county's permit materials at all — which is not the same thing as "no permit needed."

With water making up over a fifth of Arnold's area, the Critical Area clause is worth taking seriously here. We check what applies to your address before anything is scheduled.

Deck Repair FAQs for Arnold Homeowners

Why does my Arnold deck grow so much algae and mildew?

Because Arnold gives a deck both halves of the problem at once: heavy tree canopy across the Broadneck Peninsula and humidity near 75% from August through October. Leaf and pine-needle debris blocks sunlight and compounds algae and mildew growth, and horizontal deck boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it. Green film is a wet-shade problem and black streaking is mildew. Both are slip hazards, and both mean the surface is holding moisture long enough for rot to start underneath.

Can power washing fix a green, blotchy deck?

It is the right first step, but only at the right pressure. Too much pressure permanently furs the wood fibers, which makes the surface rougher, holds more water and traps more dirt — so the deck looks worse a year later than it did before. The proper sequence is cleaning or stripping at controlled pressure, letting the wood dry properly, sanding where needed, then sealing or staining. Finish applied to damp wood fails within a season, which is a large part of why refinishing jobs around here do not last.

Do I need a permit to repair a deck in Arnold?

Anne Arundel County is the only county around Baltimore that separates attached from detached decks. An attached deck always requires a permit, whatever its size or height. A detached deck is exempt only if it is under 200 square feet AND under 3 feet high AND outside the Critical Area — all three conditions together. Board-only replacement is exempt as ordinary repair. Inspections run footing, framing and final, and the county works to the 2021 International Residential Code.

My composite deck is stained and mold-spotted. Isn't composite maintenance-free?

Not entirely, and older composite in particular. A 2009 lawsuit, Mahan v. Trex, alleged that pre-2009 composite decking was susceptible to mold, mildew, fungus, spotting and surface flaking; Trex denied the allegations and the case settled for $8.25 million with product replacement plus partial labor reimbursement. That was an allegation and a settlement, not a proven defect — but if you have a mid-2000s composite deck in deep shade in Arnold, the surface problems you are seeing are not unusual and they are usually fixable.

My deck is near a riverside slope. Does that change anything?

It changes the footings and the posts. On the cliffs and slopes above the Severn and Magothy, decks stand higher above grade, stair runs get longer, and stringers and post bases sit in ground that stays damp under tree cover. The code requires a one-inch standoff above concrete for weather- and splash-exposed post bases, and wood within six inches of exposed ground must be ground-contact rated. Guards matter more too: a guard is required over 30 inches above grade, at least 36 inches high on a house, with no gap a 4-inch sphere can pass through.
