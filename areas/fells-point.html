Fells Point has the oldest and most constrained building stock of any neighborhood we work in — rowhomes running from the 1790s through the 1920s in its Federal-style core. A rooftop deck on a house that age is not a decking project with a view; it is a structure bearing on centuries-old masonry shared with the neighbors on both sides. The Deck Repair Wizard repairs those decks, and we start with what is holding them up rather than with what you walk on.

What a Fells Point rooftop deck is actually sitting on

Here is the thing most homeowners — and a surprising number of contractors — get wrong. A Baltimore rowhome rooftop deck typically spans party wall to party wall on shared masonry. It does not hang from a wood ledger bolted through a rim joist. Every prescriptive deck detail in the residential code, every span table a deck builder works from, every ledger-flashing detail: all of it assumes the ledger arrangement that does not exist up here.

That is why Baltimore City requires construction documents for rooftop structures to be signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland. On Fells Point's building stock the requirement earns its keep — the masonry is older, the footprints are narrower, and the roof assemblies underneath were built to keep weather out, not to carry people, furniture and a party. A repair approach that is routine on a 1910 rowhome a mile east may be the wrong answer on a house a hundred years older.

It is also why the party-wall requirement is not a formality. Adjoining property owners must be notified in writing, with evidence of that notice submitted alongside the construction documents. Your deck bears on walls you share. The city wants the neighbors to know before anyone starts.

Why Fells Point roof decks are failing now

The rooftop decks on these old buildings are mostly new by comparison — installed during the rowhome renovation wave that ran through the 2000s and 2010s. Which means they are now 10 to 20-plus years old, right at the age where the assemblies that keep them working stop working.

  • The waterproofing membrane is the number one failure. Blistering, alligatoring, and cracking driven by ultraviolet exposure and thermal movement. Baltimore supplies roughly 45 inches of rain a year — the sum of the National Weather Service's 1991–2020 monthly normals at BWI — plus around 30 thunderstorm days annually and a winter that crosses back and forth over freezing repeatedly.
  • Every penetration is a candidate. Post bases and curbs passing through the deck have to be flashed. An unflashed or poorly flashed penetration leaks years before the membrane itself goes.
  • The wrong system, installed cheaply. A standard roofing membrane used where a pedestrian deck waterproofing system belongs cracks within a couple of seasons under foot traffic and point loads.
  • Sleepers and framing over the roof. Wood held against a damp surface, with humidity near 75% from August through October and a freeze-free season over 230 days, rots from the bottom up while the boards above stay firm underfoot.
  • Railings. Guards over 30 inches above the walking surface must be at least 36 inches high on a home, with no opening a 4-inch sphere can pass through. Fifteen winters of movement loosens post connections quietly.

The fact that changes the math: once a deck sits on a roof, replacing the membrane underneath requires removing the deck. Early, a leak is a flashing repair. Late, it is a tear-off, a new membrane and a rebuild. On Fells Point's building stock, "late" is considerably more expensive than that sentence makes it sound.

CHAP approval and Baltimore City permit rules

Fells Point is a CHAP historic district, so the order of operations is fixed: CHAP approval comes before the building permit. Not alongside. Not after. Applying for the permit first is how these projects lose months.

  • Rooftop decks are not banned, but they are often disallowed or strictly limited, and they are reviewed for street visibility. No deck may be visible from the front of the structure; a deck visible from the rear alley may be approvable.
  • Stair penthouses are discouraged — internal stair or hatch access is preferred.
  • Raw unpainted pressure-treated lumber is generally not approved, which matters when visible boards or railing components are being replaced.
  • Baltimore City has no deck exemption at all. Decks are absent from the city's list of work exempt from permit — no square-footage threshold and no height threshold, unlike Baltimore County's 16-inch or 120-square-foot trigger.
  • Rooftop structure limits: no deck raised more than 2 feet above the plane midway between the lowest and highest points of the roof surface; 8 feet back from the face of the building wall on a flat roof, or 6 feet behind the ridge on a gabled or mansard roof; no side or rear setback required.
  • Inspections are performed by the city — footing and final.

Rooftop Deck FAQs for Fells Point Homeowners

Why does a Fells Point roof deck need drawings from an architect or engineer?

Because the deck is not hanging off a rim joist — it is bearing on masonry walls you share with your neighbors, and on buildings that in Fells Point can date to the 1790s. Baltimore rowhome rooftop decks typically span party wall to party wall on shared masonry rather than attaching to a wood ledger, which is the arrangement every prescriptive deck detail in the residential code assumes. The city requires construction documents for rooftop structures to be signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland. On the oldest building stock in the city that is not paperwork for its own sake — it is someone qualified confirming the load path is real before people stand on it.

What does the party-wall notification requirement mean in practice?

Adjoining property owners must be notified in writing, and evidence of that notice is submitted with the construction documents. On a Fells Point block where houses share walls on both sides, that means the neighbors on either side of you are formally part of the process. It is worth handling early and handling well: the notification is a requirement of the submission, so a project that skips it or handles it sloppily stalls at exactly the wrong moment. It also tends to surface the practical questions — access, staging, noise — before they become disputes.

Does the age of a Fells Point building change how a rooftop deck is repaired?

Substantially. Fells Point rowhomes run from the 1790s through the 1920s in its Federal-style core, which is the oldest and most constrained building stock anywhere in the city. Older masonry, narrower footprints and roof assemblies that were never designed for occupancy all mean less room for the standard answer. A repair that would be routine on a 1910 Canton rowhome may not be appropriate three blocks away on a house a century older. That is why we inspect and specify per building here rather than working from a template.

Is CHAP approval required before the permit in Fells Point?

Yes. Fells Point is a CHAP historic district, and CHAP approval is required before the building permit — not at the same time and not afterward. Rooftop decks are not banned in the district, but they are often disallowed or strictly limited and they are reviewed for street visibility, with the governing principle that no deck may be visible from the front of the structure. A deck visible from the rear alley may be approvable. Stair penthouses are discouraged in favor of internal stair or hatch access, and raw unpainted pressure-treated lumber is generally not approved.

My Fells Point roof deck feels solid. Why would I have it inspected?

Because on a rooftop deck the two things that fail first are both invisible from the walking surface. The waterproofing membrane under the deck is the number one failure — blistering, alligatoring, and cracking from ultraviolet exposure and thermal movement — and the sleepers and framing resting on the roof rot from the bottom up while the boards on top still look and feel fine. Rooftop decks installed during the 2000s and 2010s renovation wave are now 10 to 20-plus years old, which is squarely when those components give out. The cost difference between finding a flashing problem early and finding it after the ceiling stains is the whole argument for looking.
