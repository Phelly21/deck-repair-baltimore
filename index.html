Why Baltimore-area decks fail when they do

Most decks in this market went up during the 1980s through the 2000s, bolted onto houses that were built decades earlier. In Lutherville-Timonium the typical house dates to around 1958, and the deck out back was added later — which puts a lot of local decks at 20 to 40 years old. That's exactly the age where boards are still serviceable but the connections, the framing and the finish start giving out.

Maryland's climate does the rest. Baltimore averages roughly 45 inches of rain a year and swings above and below freezing repeatedly through the winter, so water works into every joint, freezes, and pries it open a little wider each season. Humidity sits near 75% from August through October, which is why the shaded, north-facing half of a deck grows algae while the sunny half fades. Horizontal deck boards hold standing water while vertical surfaces shed it — that's why the walking surface always fails first.

The part that matters most is where you can't see it. The ledger board — the piece bolting your deck to the house — is the most failure-prone component on any deck, and decks from the 1980s and 1990s were routinely attached with nails instead of bolts, with no flashing to keep water out of the rim joist. Here's what that failure looks like and how to check yours.

Check Your Deck

Six Signs Your Deck Needs Attention Now

The industry group NADRA estimates that roughly 30 million of the 60-plus million decks in the United States are past their recommended service life, and that fewer than half were built to code. These are the signs that matter most.

1. Movement at the house

A gap opening between the deck and the house, or any sway when you walk, points at the ledger connection — the single most failure-prone part of a deck.

2. Soft wood at the base

Press a screwdriver into post bases and stair stringers. If it sinks in, that wood is gone. Posts and stringers resting directly on soil rot from the bottom up.

3. Railings that move

Lean on the railing. If it flexes, the post connections have loosened or rotted. Guards are required to be at least 36 inches high on homes, with no gap a 4-inch sphere can pass through.

4. Rust stains and popped fasteners

Black or rust streaks around screws and bolts mean the fasteners are corroding and the wood around them is staying wet.

5. Water beading has stopped

Splash water on the boards in spring. If it soaks in instead of beading, the finish is done and the wood is now absorbing every rain.

6. Green or black film

Green algae is a wet-shade problem; black streaking is mildew. Both are slip hazards and both signal that the surface is holding moisture.

Know Before You Build

Maryland Deck Permit Rules Change at Every County Line

Most homeowners have heard that decks under 30 inches don't need a permit. In the Baltimore metro that's wrong more often than it's right, and the differences are bigger than people expect:

  • Baltimore County requires a permit for any deck more than 16 inches above grade or larger than 120 square feet — with no exemption in a historic district, floodplain or Critical Area.
  • Howard County exempts only decks under 25 square feet, but treats board-only replacement as ordinary repair with no permit.
  • Anne Arundel County is the only one that separates attached from detached: an attached deck always needs a permit, whatever its size.
  • Baltimore City has no deck size or height exemption at all, and rooftop decks carry their own rules.

We tell you which rules apply to your address before work starts — and we'll say plainly when a repair doesn't trigger a permit at all.

Common Questions

Baltimore Deck Repair FAQs

How much does deck repair cost in the Baltimore area?

Nationally, Fixr puts deck repair at $500–$3,000 with an average near $1,500, and Bob Vila reports a typical range of $859–$3,321. Baltimore-area pricing depends on deck height, access, how much framing is affected and the finish you choose. Small jobs like popped fasteners or a few cracked boards sit at the low end; structural work on joists, posts or a failing ledger runs higher. We quote in writing after an on-site look, free. See the full cost breakdown by repair type.

Is it cheaper to repair or replace a deck?

Repair is almost always cheaper when the framing is sound. If the joists, beams, posts and ledger are solid and the damage is limited to decking boards, railings or finish, repair typically costs a fraction of replacement. Replacement starts to make sense when framing rot is widespread, the structure can't be brought up to current code, or repair costs approach half the price of a new deck. Here's how we make that call.

How do I know if my deck is unsafe?

Check where the deck attaches to the house — that ledger connection is the most failure-prone part of a deck, especially on 1980s and 1990s decks fastened with nails instead of bolts. Other warning signs: a deck that bounces or sways, railings that move when you lean on them, soft or spongy wood at post bases and stair stringers, rust stains around fasteners, and gaps opening between the deck and the house. If you see any of those, stop using the deck and get it inspected.

Do you build new decks?

We specialize in repair and restoration. Most Baltimore-area deck companies sell new construction, which means a repair call often turns into a replacement quote. We do the opposite: we fix what can be fixed, and we tell you honestly when a deck is past saving instead of selling you a rebuild you don't need.

Do I need a permit to repair a deck in Maryland?

It depends on your county, and the rules genuinely differ. Baltimore County requires a permit for decks more than 16 inches above grade or larger than 120 square feet — far stricter than the 30-inch rule most homeowners assume. Howard County exempts board-only replacement as ordinary repair. Anne Arundel County always requires a permit for an attached deck, regardless of size. Baltimore City has no deck exemption at all. We'll tell you which rules apply to your address before we start.
