Deck staining and sealing protects wood from the two things that destroy it: water and sunlight. A sealer handles the water. Pigment handles the sun — which is why the more pigment a finish carries, the longer it lasts and the less wood grain you see. That single trade-off drives every decision on this page, and it is the thing most homeowners are never told before they pick a can. Get the finish type, the prep and the timing right and a deck holds for years; get any one of them wrong and it fails in a season regardless of what the label promised.

We are a repair company first, which changes how we approach finishing. We look at the deck before we look at the stain: a finish applied over soft boards, popped fasteners or a loose railing just seals in the problem and hides it from you for another year.

Sealer or stain — which does my deck need?

A sealer is a water repellent with little or no pigment. It slows water absorption and does almost nothing about UV. A stain carries pigment, and pigment is the UV block. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory documents that wood is rapidly degraded by short-wavelength UV, with further subsurface degradation from longer-wavelength violet and visible light — so a clear-sealed deck still goes grey, it just does not soak up rain while it happens. Many products do both jobs at once, so the real question is how much pigment you are willing to trade for grain.

The one-way door

Solid-colour stain is close to permanent as a decision. Once a film-forming solid is on a deck, going back to a semi-transparent look means stripping and sanding the whole surface — a full restoration, not a recoat. It is a good choice for a weathered deck. It is a poor first choice for a deck you would rather see the grain of.

Why does the deck floor always fail first?

Because horizontal boards hold standing water and vertical faces shed it. Rain sits on the walking surface, works into the grain and around the fastener heads, and stays. The same rain runs straight off a railing post. On top of that, foot traffic abrades the floor finish while nothing touches the railings, and leaf and pine-needle debris collects on the deck surface, blocking sunlight and compounding algae and mildew growth. That is the reason solid stain gives 1 to 3 years on a floor and 3 to 7 years on a railing — the same product, different jobs.

The practical takeaway: a maintenance plan that recoats the floor on a shorter cycle than the railings is cheaper and looks better than waiting for the whole deck to fail at once.

How do I know when it's time to re-stain?

Run the water-bead test every spring, before the season starts.

  1. Pick three spots: a main traffic path, a spot under a chair or table, and a section that gets full afternoon sun.
  2. Splash a cup of water on each.
  3. Watch for a minute or two.
  4. Beading = the finish is still working. Wait and check again next spring.
  5. Soaking in and darkening = the finish is done. The deck is absorbing rain, and every day it sits bare is a day of UV damage.

Other signs it is due: colour faded unevenly along the traffic lanes, grey showing through the tint, a chalky feel underfoot, and algae or mildew returning fast after a cleaning. Our guide to how often to stain a deck in Maryland covers the intervals in more depth.

When is the best time to stain a deck in Maryland?

Late spring and early fall. Both give moderate temperatures and the lowest practical humidity of the outdoor season. The local numbers behind that:

  • Humidity runs near 60% February through April and climbs to roughly 75% August through October (Maryland State Climatologist) — high humidity slows drying both before and after application.
  • July average high 88.8°F, August 86.5°F (NWS BWI). Hot boards flash the carrier off a finish before it can soak in, leaving a coat that sits on top and peels.
  • 30 thunderstorm days a year in eastern Maryland, peaking in July. A finish caught by a storm in its first hours is a finish you pay for twice.
  • Roughly 45 inches of rain a year (the sum of NWS BWI 1991–2020 monthly normals) and a freeze-free season over 230 days — a long working season, but a wet one.

Read the full Maryland staining-season guide for the month-by-month version. The rule we work by: we would rather move your finish day than put stain on damp wood.

Does the wood species change the finish?

Yes, significantly. The decks we work on around the metro are mostly pressure-treated southern yellow pine, with western red cedar, Ipe and mahogany (red balau) turning up regularly, plus composite that should never be stained at all.

  • Pressure-treated southern yellow pine — the default here. Takes pigment well once clean and dry. Service life runs about 10 to 15 years with maintenance per Southern Pine Council guidance.
  • Western red cedar — soft, beautiful, and easy to damage with too much pressure during prep. Usually finished transparent or semi-transparent.
  • Ipe and mahogany — dense tropical hardwoods that resist absorption. They need hardwood-specific products and more frequent maintenance on the horizontal surfaces.
  • Composite — do not stain it. Composite needs cleaning and, where it is damaged, composite and Trex repair.

Deck Staining & Sealing FAQs

What is the difference between staining and sealing a deck?

A sealer is a water repellent with little or no pigment: it slows water absorption but does almost nothing about sunlight. A stain carries pigment, and that pigment is what blocks UV. Since wood is rapidly degraded by short-wavelength UV, a clear sealer keeps water out for a while but lets the surface grey anyway. That is why the longest-lasting finishes are the most pigmented ones. Many products do both jobs in one can, which is why the terms get used interchangeably — the practical question is how much pigment you want, not whether you want protection.

How long does deck stain last in Maryland?

It depends on the pigment level and on whether you are asking about the floor or the railings. Water-repellent sealers generally need reapplication every 12 to 18 months, and sometimes every 6 to 12 months on a deck in full sun with heavy traffic. Transparent and semi-transparent stains typically last 12 to 18 months on horizontal surfaces. Solid-colour stain runs about 1 to 3 years on horizontal surfaces and 3 to 7 years on vertical ones. Horizontal boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it, which is why the walking surface always comes due first.

When is the best time of year to stain a deck in Maryland?

Late spring and early fall are the sweet spots, because both offer moderate temperatures and lower humidity than midsummer. Humidity in Maryland sits near 60 percent from February through April and climbs to roughly 75 percent from August through October, and eastern Maryland averages 30 thunderstorm days a year with a July peak. Add July average highs near 89 degrees, which can flash off a finish before it soaks in, and midsummer afternoons are the worst window of the year. We schedule the finish day on the forecast, and we will move it rather than put stain on damp wood.

How do I know when my deck needs re-staining?

Do the water-bead test each spring. Splash a cup of water on several spots on the walking surface, including a traffic path and a spot under a chair. If the water beads up, the finish is still working. If it soaks in and darkens the wood within a minute or two, the finish is finished and the deck is absorbing every rainfall. Other signs: colour fading unevenly along the traffic paths, grey patches showing through, a chalky feel underfoot, and green or black growth returning quickly after cleaning.

Can you stain over an old finish?

Sometimes, and it depends entirely on what is on there now. A thinning penetrant finish can usually be cleaned and recoated with a compatible product. A film-forming solid-colour stain that is peeling or flaking has to be stripped, because anything applied over a lifting film lifts with it. Mixing types is the classic failure: an oil finish over a water-based film, or a clear sealer over a solid stain, can leave you with a surface that never cures properly. We test a section before we quote, and if stripping is needed we say so up front.

Why does the deck floor fail before the railings?

Foot traffic abrades the floor finish while nothing touches the railing, and leaf and pine-needle debris collects on the deck surface, blocking sunlight and compounding algae and mildew growth. That is why solid-colour stain lasts 1 to 3 years on a deck floor and 3 to 7 years on the railings, and why a smart maintenance plan recoats the floor more often than the rest.
