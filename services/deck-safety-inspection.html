What is a deck safety inspection, and is it worth doing?

A deck safety inspection is a structured check of every component that holds your deck up, done in the order those components actually fail — the ledger connection first, then framing, posts and footings, hardware, guards and stairs, with the walking surface last. Ours is free, and you walk away with photos and a written punch list whether or not you hire us for anything. If your deck is more than about ten years old and has never been looked at below the boards, it is worth doing this spring.

It's worth doing because the failures that matter are invisible from a deck chair. The industry group NADRA estimates that roughly 30 million of the 60-plus million decks in the United States are past their recommended service life and that fewer than half were built to code. A deck that looks fine and a deck that is fine are two different decks.

Why our inspection is free

Because we only do repairs. A deck builder inspecting your deck has a new deck to sell; a home inspector has a report to sell and no way to fix anything. We want the repair work, so the inspection is how we earn the chance at it — and if the deck turns out to be in good shape, telling you that costs us nothing but a morning.

What we check, in the order decks actually fail

Deck industry and home-inspection groups agree on the ranking: ledger board, beams, support posts, hardware connections, railings, stairs. We work that list.

  • 1. The ledger connection. Is it bolted or nailed? Is there continuous flashing at the ledger-to-house joint, as the code requires? Are there lateral load hold-downs — the code calls for 1,500 pounds of tension resistance at each end, either two devices rated 1,500 pounds or more within 24 inches of each deck end or four rated 750 pounds or more, in addition to the ledger bolts. And what is the ledger attached to? The code does not accept cantilevered floor joists, brick or stone veneer, or an engineered I-joist rim without the manufacturer's reinforcement.
  • 2. Beam and joists. Probed for rot at the ends and along the top edge, checked for sag, and checked for spans that exceed what the lumber can carry.
  • 3. Posts and footings. Post bases probed, standoff checked — the code calls for a one-inch standoff above concrete on weather- and splash-exposed post bases — and footings assessed against the 30-inch frost depth Baltimore County specifies.
  • 4. Hardware and fasteners. Joist hangers for correct fasteners and corrosion, bolts for rust streaking. Baltimore County specifies triple-dip galvanized or stainless hardware; ordinary fasteners corrode quickly against modern treated lumber.
  • 5. Guards and railings. Every post load-tested by hand, guard height measured against the 36-inch minimum for homes, and openings checked against the 4-inch sphere rule.
  • 6. Stairs. Stringer bottoms probed, bearing checked, risers and treads measured against the 7¾-inch maximum rise and 10-inch minimum tread, and handrails checked for the 34-to-38-inch height and graspable profile required at four or more risers.
  • 7. Surface and finish. Boards probed top and underside, and a water-bead test on the finish — if water soaks in rather than beading, the deck is absorbing every rain.

Signs you should book an inspection now rather than in spring

  • The deck bounces or sways when you walk on it.
  • A gap has opened between the deck and the house, or the siding below the deck is stained or peeling.
  • A screwdriver sinks into wood at a post base, a joist end or a stair stringer.
  • A railing moves when you lean against it.
  • Rust streaks run down from bolts or joist hangers.
  • The deck has just been through a serious storm. Maryland recorded 85 billion-dollar disasters between 1980 and 2024 according to NOAA's National Centers for Environmental Information, and recent years have averaged 5.6 such events annually against a long-run average of 1.9.
  • You are buying or selling the house and nobody knows how old the deck is or who built it.
  • You are about to put a crowd on it. A deck is designed for 40 pounds per square foot of live load — a party concentrates far more than the average.

What the inspection leads to

Most punch lists point at one of a handful of repairs. The common ones:

  • Deck structural and rot repair — rotted joists, a nailed or unflashed ledger, sagging framing, posts and footings. The highest-stakes item on any list.
  • Deck board replacement — soft, cracked or cupped boards, or a full resurface over sound framing.
  • Railing repair and code retrofit — loose posts, short guards, and baluster gaps wider than 4 inches.
  • Stair and step repair — stringers on soil, uneven bottom risers, missing handrails.
  • Staining and sealing — when the water-bead test fails. Water-repellent sealers typically need reapplication every 12 to 18 months, and sometimes every 6 to 12 under heavy sun and traffic.

Costs for those repairs are published only as national averages — there is no Maryland-specific deck pricing data — and we lay all of them out, source by source, in the Baltimore deck repair cost guide so you can sanity-check any quote you're given, including ours.

Deck Inspection FAQs

How much does a deck inspection cost?

Ours is free, and there is no obligation attached to it. You get a walk-through of what we find, photos of the problem areas, and a written punch list you can keep — including the items we think can wait and the ones we think cannot. If you want repairs, we quote them in writing. If you want to take the list to someone else, or do the small items yourself, that is entirely fine.

What does a deck safety inspection actually check?

We follow the order in which decks actually fail. The ledger connection at the house comes first — bolts versus nails, flashing, lateral hold-downs and what the ledger is attached to. Then the beam and joists, then the posts and footings, then the hardware and fasteners, then the guards and railings, then the stairs, and finally the decking surface and finish. We probe wood at the joist ends, post bases and stair stringers rather than judging it by eye, because rot is usually well advanced before it is visible.

How often should a deck be inspected?

Once a year is sensible for a deck over about ten years old, and spring is the natural time — the industry runs Deck Safety Month in May for exactly that reason. Inspect sooner if your deck has just been through a serious storm, if you have noticed any new movement, if you are about to host a large gathering on it, or if you are buying or selling the house and the deck's age is unknown.

Why does the ledger board get checked first?

Because it is the most failure-prone component on a deck, according to both deck industry and home inspection groups, and because it carries roughly half the deck's load. Decks built in the 1980s and 1990s were commonly nailed instead of bolted and frequently had no flashing above the ledger. The code also does not accept brick or stone veneer as a structural attachment surface — a real issue on Baltimore's brick housing stock, where a ledger lagged into veneer is not attached to the house structure at all.

Can you inspect and repair the deck, or do I need two companies?

One company. People search for deck inspection and repair together because splitting them is a hassle — an inspection report from someone who cannot fix anything leaves you making a second round of calls and paying twice for the same diagnosis. We inspect, we show you what we found, and we can do the work. The difference from a deck builder is that we have no replacement to sell you, so a clean deck gets told it is a clean deck.

How do I know if my deck is unsafe right now?

Stop using it if the deck sways or bounces, if a gap has opened between the deck and the house, if a screwdriver sinks into wood at a post base or stair stringer, or if a railing moves when you lean on it. The US Consumer Product Safety Commission recorded roughly 224,000 people injured in deck and porch incidents between 2003 and 2007, about 33,000 of them from structural failure or collapse. Those symptoms are how a failing deck announces itself beforehand.

What happens after the inspection?

You get the written punch list, sorted into what is urgent, what should be scheduled, and what is cosmetic. If repairs make sense we quote them itemized, and we tell you whether your county requires a permit for the scope described — the rules differ significantly between Baltimore County, Howard, Anne Arundel, Harford, Carroll and Baltimore City. And if the deck is genuinely past saving, we say so plainly rather than quoting a repair we do not believe in.
