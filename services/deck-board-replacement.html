What is deck board replacement — and do you need it or a whole new deck?

Deck board replacement means pulling the failed decking off your existing frame and installing new boards in their place, either in spots or across the whole surface. If your deck's joists, beam, posts and ledger are sound and the problem is the part you walk on, this is the repair you need — not a new deck. It is the single most common reason people call us, and the single most common repair that gets quoted as a replacement by somebody else.

There is a straightforward physical reason the surface fails first: horizontal boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it. Add leaf and pine-needle debris that blocks sunlight and traps moisture, and the top of your deck absorbs a beating the framing never sees. On a lot of 25-year-old Baltimore-area decks, the boards are shot and the frame underneath is fine.

Signs your deck boards need replacing

  • A screwdriver sinks into the board. Soft or spongy wood is gone; no amount of sanding or stain brings it back.
  • Cracks running the length of the board or splitting out through a fastener hole.
  • Cupping. The board has curled into a shallow trough that now holds water after every rain, which accelerates everything else.
  • Splintering that keeps coming back after sanding — the surface fibers have broken down from years of ultraviolet exposure. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory documents that wood is rapidly degraded by short-wavelength UV, with additional subsurface degradation from longer-wavelength light.
  • Soft undersides. Reach under an accessible board and press. The underside dries slowest and often fails before the top shows anything.
  • Boards that flex or creak underfoot — sometimes the board, sometimes the joist beneath it, which is why we check both.
  • Fasteners that won't stay put. Popped screws and backed-out nails mean the wood around them has lost its grip.
  • Black or green film that returns within weeks of cleaning, a sign the board is holding moisture rather than shedding it.

Partial replacement or full resurfacing — which do you need?

Two different jobs, and the difference is money.

  • Partial board replacement is right when fewer than roughly a quarter of the boards have failed and the rest still have life. We pull the bad ones, inspect the joists underneath, replace what needs replacing and blend the new boards into the field.
  • Full resurfacing — sometimes called re-decking — strips the entire walking surface and usually the railing, then installs a new surface on the existing frame. It costs far less than a new deck because the footings, posts, beam and joists stay put, and in most counties the permit picture is simpler as a result.

The line between them is not aesthetic. If we lift boards and find joists that have lost section, hangers that have corroded through, or a ledger that was nailed rather than bolted, the honest answer is that the frame comes first — see deck structural and rot repair. Putting a beautiful new surface on a failing frame is the most expensive mistake homeowners make with a deck.

What's included in a board replacement

  1. Free inspection and board count. We probe the decking, check the framing beneath, and tell you whether you're looking at eight boards or a resurface.
  2. Written estimate with the board species, fastening method and finish plan spelled out, plus whether your county requires a permit for the scope described.
  3. Removal. Failed boards come up and the joist tops are exposed and inspected — this is the only moment in a deck's life when the framing is easy to see, so we use it.
  4. Joist preparation. Any soft joist tops are addressed. Where we're re-decking, joist tape over the joist tops is worth discussing, because most joist rot starts at the fastener holes on the top edge.
  5. Installation. New boards set with consistent spacing for drainage and expansion, fastened with hardware rated for contact with modern treated lumber. Hidden fastener systems are an option on grooved composite and some hardwoods; face-screwing is the workhorse on pressure-treated pine.
  6. Trim, edges and transitions so the repair doesn't read as a patch at the stair landing or the ledger.
  7. Finish scheduling. New treated lumber arrives wet. We schedule staining and sealing once the wood is dry enough to take it, rather than sealing in the moisture.

Does board replacement need a permit around Baltimore?

Only two jurisdictions here address it directly, and the difference is worth knowing before you call anyone:

  • Howard County treats board-only replacement as ordinary repair — no permit.
  • Anne Arundel County likewise requires no permit for board-only replacement.
  • Baltimore County, Harford, Carroll and Baltimore City do not address board-only replacement in their published materials. Don't assume parity. Baltimore County's general trigger is any deck more than 16 inches above grade or over 120 square feet, with no exemption in a historic district, floodplain or Critical Area, and Baltimore City publishes no deck exemption at all.

If you're in a Columbia village there is a second layer: every property is under recorded covenants, and exterior alterations go through the village architectural committee before work starts. Our Howard County permit and Columbia village approval guide walks through both, and the Baltimore County deck permit guide covers the 16-inch rule most homeowners have never heard of.

Why hire a local pro for deck boards?

Because the boards are the easy part and the joists are the point. Pulling decking is the one opportunity you get to see the framing without paying for demolition — a crew that just screws new boards down over whatever is there has thrown that opportunity away, and you will pay for it in five years when the fasteners start pulling out of soft joist tops.

Common Questions

Deck Board Replacement FAQs

How much does deck board replacement cost?

Treat every published figure as national, because no Baltimore-specific pricing exists. Nationally, Bob Vila puts a board replacement project at $500–$4,000 and floor board repair at $10–$35 per square foot; Fixr puts board replacement at $15–$55 per square foot; and resurfacing an entire deck runs $15–$50 per square foot. Materials alone range from $3 to $45 per square foot depending on species, with cedar at $25–$55 and composite at $12–$45 installed. Your local number depends on board species, deck height, how many joists need attention once the boards are off, and how much of the surface is being replaced.

Can you replace just a few deck boards instead of all of them?

Yes, and it is usually the right call. If the framing is sound and only a handful of boards have gone soft, cracked or cupped, we pull those boards, check the joists underneath, and set new ones. The catch is appearance: a new board next to a twenty-year-old board will not match until it has weathered and been refinished. We plan the layout so replacements fall where they are least obvious, and we finish the whole surface afterward so it reads as one deck rather than a patch.

What is deck resurfacing and is it cheaper than a new deck?

Resurfacing means removing the entire walking surface and railing and installing a new one on the existing frame. It is substantially cheaper than a new deck because the expensive, permit-heavy parts — footings, posts, beam and joists — stay where they are. It is only appropriate when the frame is genuinely sound, which is why we inspect and probe the framing before we ever quote a resurface. Putting a new surface on a failing frame is the single most expensive mistake in this business.

How do I know a deck board needs replacing rather than sanding?

Sanding fixes surface problems: splinters, furring from over-pressured washing, and raised grain. It does not fix structure. Replace the board when a screwdriver sinks into it, when a crack runs the full length or through a fastener, when the board has cupped enough to hold standing water, when the ends around fasteners have crumbled, or when the underside is soft even though the top looks fine. That last one is common — the underside stays damp far longer than the top.

Do I need a permit to replace deck boards in Maryland?

It depends on the county, and only two are explicit. Howard County treats board-only replacement as ordinary repair with no permit, and Anne Arundel County also requires no permit for board-only replacement. Baltimore County, Harford County, Carroll County and Baltimore City do not address board-only replacement in their published permit materials, so do not assume parity — Baltimore County's general trigger is any deck more than 16 inches above grade or over 120 square feet, and Baltimore City publishes no deck exemption at all. We confirm the requirement for your address before work is scheduled.

What boards should I use for a replacement in Maryland's climate?

Pressure-treated southern yellow pine is the default and the value choice; the Southern Pine Council's industry range puts it at roughly 10 to 15 years with maintenance, up to 15 to 25 years above ground when every field cut is resealed, and under 10 years when it is never sealed. Western red cedar looks better and costs more but is unforgiving about ground contact. Ipe and mahogany are extremely durable but need pre-drilling and stainless fasteners. Composite eliminates rot at the surface but does nothing for the treated-pine framing below it, which keeps rotting on its own schedule.

Will the new boards match my existing deck?

Not immediately. New pressure-treated lumber arrives wet and light, and it needs to dry before it will take stain evenly — putting finish on damp boards is the fastest way to a finish that fails in one season. We match species, width and profile as closely as the original allows, set the new boards with consistent spacing and fastening, and then schedule the refinish once the wood is ready so the whole deck comes out one color.
