Composite repair kits — filler, epoxy, putty, colour-matched sticks — fix cosmetic dings in low-traffic corners and very little else. If the patch you applied is lifting, showing, or the spot came back, the kit was never the right tool. Composite boards expand and contract with temperature while cured filler does not, so a patch works loose at its edges; the colour is an approximation on day one and drifts further as the surrounding board keeps weathering; and a filler does nothing about the reason the board failed, which is usually trapped moisture or wood framing moving underneath. The fix that lasts is board replacement, done one board at a time without tearing out the deck.

That is the business we are in. Every deck company in this market sells new decks — and a composite deck with three bad boards is an easy rebuild pitch, because the boards are the expensive part. We would rather replace three boards.

When won't a repair kit cut it?

  • The damage is in a traffic path. Feet, furniture and grills find a filled spot and work it loose.
  • The board has cracked through or split at a fastener. That is a structural failure of the board; filler is cosmetic.
  • The spot came back after cleaning. If mold or spotting returns, the board is still holding moisture — patching seals the symptom in.
  • The board flexes or feels soft underfoot. That is almost never the board. That is a joist, and it needs structural and rot repair.
  • You can see the patch. Composite colour is a manufactured blend; filler is a single tone. On a surface you look at every day, it shows.
  • Several boards are involved. Once you are patching a fourth spot, replacement is cheaper and looks finished.

What goes wrong with composite decks?

Composite was sold as maintenance-free. It is lower-maintenance than wood, which is not the same thing — and the older generations especially have a track record.

The Trex history, stated accurately

Mahan v. Trex, filed in 2009, alleged that composite decking manufactured before 2009 was susceptible to mold, mildew, fungus, spotting and surface flaking. Trex denied the allegations and the case settled for $8.25 million, with product replacement plus partial labour reimbursement for class members. That is an allegation resolved by settlement — not a proven defect, and we will not characterise it as one. The useful takeaway for a homeowner: if your composite deck dates to the mid-2000s and it is spotting or flaking, that behaviour is documented and you are not imagining it. Trex itself is headquartered in Winchester, Virginia, about two hours from Baltimore, which makes it one of the more common composite brands on decks around this metro.

How do you replace one composite board?

  1. Identify the system. Face-screwed, grooved-edge with hidden clips, or a proprietary track. This determines everything about how the board comes out.
  2. Source the match. We check brand, profile and colour, and tell you honestly how close we can get. A board made today will not match one that has weathered fifteen years in the sun.
  3. Release the fasteners. With hidden clips, the neighbouring boards have to be freed on both sides of the one coming out.
  4. Remove the board and inspect the joists. This is the only time you get to see the framing under that section. We look at joist condition, fastener corrosion and blocking while it is open.
  5. Repair the framing if it needs it. A new board over a failing joist is a waste of a new board.
  6. Set the new board with correct gapping for thermal movement — composite moves more than wood, and boards set tight buckle.
  7. Re-secure the neighbours with new clips where the old ones are fatigued, and check the run for level and alignment.
  8. Placement strategy where the colour won't match. Sometimes the better answer is moving a weathered board from an out-of-sight area — under a bench, at the house wall — into the visible spot and putting the new board where nobody looks.

Don't forget what's holding it up

The single biggest blind spot on composite decks: the boards are composite, but the joists, beams, posts and ledger are almost always pressure-treated wood, and they age like any other deck frame. A flawless composite surface can sit on twenty-year-old framing with a nailed ledger and no flashing behind it.

The ledger board is the most failure-prone component on any deck, and decks built in the 1980s and 1990s were routinely attached with nails rather than bolts. The code requires continuous flashing at the ledger-to-house connection and hold-downs resisting 1,500 pounds of tension at each ledger end. NADRA estimates roughly 30 million of the 60-plus million decks in the United States are past their recommended service life and that fewer than half were built to code — and a composite surface does not exempt a deck from that list. How should a composite deck be cleaned?

Gently, and with the gaps cleared. Composite does not need stain or sealer — and should not be given any — but it does need washing, especially here. Humidity near 75% from August through October keeps shaded sections damp for weeks, and leaf and pine-needle debris blocks sunlight and compounds algae and mildew growth. Debris packed into the board gaps is the single most common cause of mold spotting we find.

  • Clear the gaps between boards every autumn — that is where the damp lives
  • Keep planters on feet so water is not trapped against the surface
  • Wash with a composite-appropriate cleaner rather than relying on pressure
  • Keep pressure low; excessive pressure can damage a composite surface as surely as it furs wood — see our deck power washing page for how we handle pressure
  • Never apply deck stain or sealer to composite; if you want colour, that is a wood deck staining conversation, not a composite one

Composite & Trex Repair FAQs

Do composite deck repair kits actually work?

For small cosmetic dings in a low-traffic spot, sometimes. For anything structural or anything you will look at every day, rarely. Filler, epoxy and putty kits fail on composite for three reasons: composite flexes and expands with temperature while cured filler does not, so the patch works loose at the edges; the colour is a compromise from day one and diverges further as the board around it weathers; and a kit does nothing about why the board failed, which on composite is often water sitting in a spot that never dries or wood framing giving way underneath. If you have already tried a kit and the patch is lifting or showing, replacing the board is usually cheaper than trying again.

Can you replace a single composite board without rebuilding the deck?

Yes, and that is most of what this service is. A composite board in the middle of a run can be removed and replaced without disturbing the boards around it. The complications are the fastening system and the colour. Many composite decks use hidden fasteners set in a groove along the board edge, so getting one board out means releasing the clips on both sides and re-securing the neighbours afterward. Colour is the other issue: a board made today will not match a board that has been weathering in the sun for fifteen years, so we discuss placement, sourcing and blending options before we start.

Why does my composite deck have mold spots?

Because older composite is not the maintenance-free product it was sold as, and because this climate is hard on any surface that stays damp. Wood-plastic composite contains wood flour, and where a board holds moisture — shaded areas, spots under planters, places where debris collects between boards — mold and mildew can take hold on and in the surface. Maryland humidity sits near 75 percent from August through October, which keeps those areas damp for weeks at a time. Clearing debris from the board gaps and keeping planters up off the surface prevents more of it than any product does.

Wasn't there a lawsuit about Trex decking?

Yes. Mahan v. Trex, filed in 2009, alleged that composite decking manufactured before 2009 was susceptible to mold, mildew, fungus, spotting and surface flaking. Trex denied the allegations and settled the case for $8.25 million, with product replacement plus partial labour reimbursement for class members. It is important to state this accurately: it was an allegation resolved by a settlement in which the company denied the claims, not a proven defect. The practical takeaway for homeowners is simply that a composite deck installed in the mid-2000s is not maintenance-free, and if yours is spotting or flaking you are not imagining it. Trex is headquartered in Winchester, Virginia, roughly a two-hour drive from Baltimore.

Can faded or scratched composite be restored?

Partly, and it depends on the generation of the product. Early uncapped composite boards fade as the surface weathers, and that fading is generally uniform enough to live with; the problem is when a new board is dropped into the middle of it. Scratches are different depending on construction: on some uncapped boards a light scratch can be blended, while a capped composite has a factory shell that cannot be sanded back without exposing the core underneath. We will tell you honestly which one you have and whether blending, board rotation or replacement is the better answer. What we will not do is sand a capped board and hope.

What does composite deck repair cost?

No Maryland-specific published cost data exists for deck work, so these are national averages. Nationally, composite deck repair runs $12 to $45 per square foot, board replacement runs $15 to $55 per square foot, and a board replacement project runs $500 to $4,000 per Bob Vila. Overall deck repair nationally sits at $500 to $3,000 with an average near $1,500 per Fixr. Popped screws run $100 to $200 and mold treatment $200 to $600. Baltimore-area pricing depends on how many boards are involved, the fastening system, whether matching material can be sourced, and the condition of the wood framing underneath.

Is the framing under a composite deck still wood?

Almost always, and this is the thing composite owners overlook. The boards are composite but the joists, beams, posts and ledger underneath are usually pressure-treated lumber, and they age exactly like any other deck frame. A composite surface can look flawless over framing that is twenty years old with a nailed ledger and no flashing — and the ledger connection is the most failure-prone part of any deck. When we are on a composite deck for a board repair, we check the frame underneath as a matter of course, because the surface tells you nothing about what is holding it up.
