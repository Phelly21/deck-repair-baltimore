What is deck stair repair, and do your stairs need it?

Deck stair repair covers everything in the flight between the deck and the ground: the treads you step on, the notched stringers that carry them, the bearing and footing at the bottom, and the guard and handrail alongside. If a tread flexes, a step feels soft, the bottom step is a different height from the rest, or the whole flight shifts when you come down it, those stairs need work now — not at the end of the season.

Stairs are worth treating as their own repair because they fail on a faster clock than the rest of the deck. Deck industry and home-inspection groups list stairs among the components that fail most often, and they note a specific reason: stair stringers are routinely set on soil or on a loose block instead of a frost-depth footing. Every other part of your deck is held up in the air. The stairs are the part standing in the dirt.

Signs your deck stairs need repair

  • A screwdriver sinks into the bottom of a stringer. This is the single most common serious defect we find on deck stairs, and it is invisible from above.
  • Treads that flex or bounce underfoot, or that have cracked along their length or split out at a fastener.
  • An uneven bottom step. Grade settles, or the stringer sinks, and the last riser ends up shorter or taller than the rest — which is exactly the condition that causes falls.
  • Stringers resting directly on soil, gravel or a patio block with no footing and no standoff.
  • Movement side to side when you walk down — the flight isn't braced or the top connection has loosened.
  • No handrail, or a flat 2x4 rail you cannot wrap a hand around.
  • Gaps in the stair guard wide enough to pass a ball through.
  • Green algae or black mildew on the treads. Stairs stay shaded and wet longer than the deck surface, and wet stair treads are the slipperiest surface on the property.

What the code requires of deck stairs

Most older decks in this market miss on at least one of these, and the misses are the reason people fall:

  • Riser height: a maximum of 7¾ inches.
  • Tread depth: a minimum of 10 inches.
  • Consistency: risers within a flight have to be uniform. An uneven bottom step created by settling is both a code problem and the most reliable way to put someone on the ground.
  • Handrails: required on stairways with four or more risers, mounted 34 to 38 inches above the tread nosings, and graspable — a round handrail of 1¼ to 2 inches in diameter satisfies that.
  • Stair guards: where a guard is required, the triangular opening formed by the tread, riser and bottom rail is allowed a 6-inch sphere, while ordinary guard openings are limited to a 4-inch sphere. See deck railing repair and code retrofit for the full guard picture.
  • Bearing at the bottom: Baltimore County specifies footings at 30 inches for frost depth, a minimum 10-inch thickness and 3,000 psi concrete; Howard County requires frost footings at 30 inches. Wood within six inches of exposed ground or embedded in concrete has to be ground-contact rated.

The US Consumer Product Safety Commission recorded roughly 224,000 people injured in deck and porch incidents between 2003 and 2007, about 33,000 of those from structural failure or collapse. Stairs concentrate the risk: they are the one part of a deck where a failure puts you in motion and above the ground at the same time.

What a deck stair repair includes

  • Tread replacement. Soft, cracked or cupped treads pulled and replaced in material rated for the exposure, fastened so the ends don't split.
  • Stringer repair or replacement. A stringer is a notched board, so it has far less remaining section than its dimensions suggest — rot at a notch removes exactly the wood that carries load. Compromised stringers get replaced, not patched.
  • Bottom bearing and footings. Stringers lifted off soil and set on a proper footing with standoff hardware so end grain is never in contact with the ground.
  • Top connection. The stringer-to-deck connection re-made with rated hardware — Baltimore County specifies triple-dip galvanized or stainless.
  • Riser correction. Uneven bottom risers brought back into a uniform flight rather than shimmed.
  • Handrail installation at a graspable profile and the correct height, plus stair guard infill where openings are too wide.
  • Finish and traction. Refinishing through deck staining and sealing, after the surfaces have been cleaned properly — see deck power washing and stripping, which matters more on stairs than anywhere else because algae on treads is a genuine slip hazard.

How a stair repair goes

  1. Free inspection. We probe every stringer at the bottom, check the top connection, measure risers and treads through the flight, and check handrail height and graspability.
  2. Written estimate, itemized by step and by scope, with the permit question answered for your county.
  3. Frame check. The stairs hang off the deck frame, so if the framing they're attached to is soft, that comes first — see deck structural and rot repair.
  4. Demolition and footing work. Failed stringers come out and proper bearing goes in at the bottom.
  5. Rebuild. Stringers cut to a uniform rise and run, treads set, connections made with rated hardware.
  6. Guard and handrail. Installed to current height, spacing and graspability requirements.
  7. Walk-through. We walk the flight with you, then leave the workmanship warranty in writing.

Why hire a local pro for deck stairs?

Because the part of your stairs that matters most is the part in the dirt. Replacing three soft treads takes an afternoon and does nothing if the stringers carrying them are rotting from the bottom up on bare soil. The repair that lasts starts with getting the wood off the ground and onto a real footing — and knowing what your county expects of that footing.

Terrain around this metro makes stairs a bigger deal than they are in flatter markets. Common Questions

Deck Stair Repair FAQs

Why do deck stairs rot before the rest of the deck?

Because the bottom of the stringer is usually sitting in the worst possible place. Home inspection and deck industry groups note that stair stringers are frequently set directly on soil or on a loose block instead of on a frost-depth footing. Soil holds moisture against the end grain year-round, and end grain absorbs water faster than any other surface on a board. Add the fact that stair treads are horizontal and hold standing water, and the stairs take more moisture than any other part of the deck.

Do deck stairs need a handrail?

Yes, once there are four or more risers. The code requires a handrail on stairways with four or more risers, mounted between 34 and 38 inches above the nosing of the treads, and graspable — a round handrail between 1¼ and 2 inches in diameter meets that requirement. A 2x4 laid flat on top of the guard posts is extremely common on older decks and is not a graspable handrail.

What are the code dimensions for deck stair treads and risers?

The code sets a maximum riser height of 7¾ inches and a minimum tread depth of 10 inches. Just as important, risers within a flight have to be consistent — an uneven bottom step, often created when grade settles or a stringer sinks, is one of the most common causes of a fall because your foot arrives where your body did not expect the ground to be.

Can you repair just the bad steps instead of rebuilding the stairs?

Often yes. Individual treads can be replaced, a loose stringer can be re-anchored at the top, and a bottom landing can be corrected without touching the rest of the flight. The point where we stop recommending a patch is when the stringers themselves are compromised — a stringer is a notched board, so it has less wood left than it looks like, and rot at a notch takes away exactly the material that carries the load. When two or more stringers are soft, rebuilding the flight is the honest recommendation.

How much does deck stair repair cost?

Every published figure is a national average, not a Baltimore price. Nationally, both Fixr and Bob Vila put step repair at $15–$50 per step and step replacement at $30–$60 each, while Bob Vila puts a wobbly-stairs project at $200–$2,000. A new stair footing runs $300–$400 nationally and post replacement $200–$400. Locally the number depends on how many risers you have, whether the stringers survive, whether a proper footing has to be dug, and whether the guard and handrail come up to current requirements at the same time.

Do deck stairs need their own footing?

They need solid, frost-protected bearing at the bottom, and a poured footing is how that is normally achieved. Baltimore County specifies footings at 30 inches for frost depth with a minimum 10-inch thickness and 3,000 psi concrete. A stringer resting on a patio block or on bare dirt has neither frost protection nor a barrier against ground moisture, which is why that detail produces both the rot and the settling that make an uneven bottom step.

Does a stair repair need a permit?

It depends on the county, and none of them publish a stair-specific exemption. Baltimore County requires a permit for decks more than 16 inches above grade or over 120 square feet, with no exemption in a historic district, floodplain or Critical Area, and inspects footing, framing and completion. Howard County exempts only decks under 25 square feet and requires frost footings at 30 inches. Anne Arundel County always requires a permit for an attached deck. Baltimore City publishes no deck exemption at all. We confirm the requirement for your address in the written estimate.
