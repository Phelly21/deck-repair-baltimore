Deck restoration is the full process of bringing an existing deck back to a safe, clean, finished condition: strip off the failed coating and growth, repair or replace the wood and hardware that have given out, sand the surface smooth, then refinish it with a stain or sealer suited to the wood. It is the right answer for the large number of Baltimore-area decks that look finished but are not — decks where the framing underneath is still solid and only the surface, the fasteners and a handful of boards have worn out. Almost every deck company in this market sells new decks, so a call about a grey, splintery surface tends to come back as a rebuild quote. We do restoration because, most of the time, restoration is what the deck actually needs.

Timing is on the homeowner's side here. Most decks in the north and west Baltimore metro went up between the 1980s and the 2000s, bolted onto houses that were already decades old — in Lutherville-Timonium the typical house dates to around 1958. That puts a great many local decks at 20 to 40 years old: past the point where the original finish means anything, but well inside the range where pressure-treated southern yellow pine keeps serving. The Southern Pine Council's range for treated pine is roughly 10 to 15 years with maintenance and as much as 15 to 25 years above ground when every field cut is resealed, against under 10 years when a deck is never sealed at all. A restoration puts a deck back on the maintained side of that line.

How do I know my deck needs restoration and not just a coat of stain?

A recoat works when the existing finish is thinning evenly. Restoration is the answer when the surface has already failed. Look for these:

  • Water soaks in instead of beading. Splash a cup of water on the boards in spring. If it darkens the wood immediately, the finish is gone and the deck has been drinking every rainfall.
  • The colour is grey and blotchy. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory documents that wood is rapidly degraded by short-wavelength UV, with additional subsurface damage from violet and visible light. Grey is that degradation, and it has to be removed rather than covered.
  • Splinters and raised grain. Bare feet find these before you do. Raised, furry grain means the surface fibers have broken down and need sanding, not coating.
  • Green film in the shade, black streaks in the damp. Green is algae, black is mildew. Both are slip hazards and both mean the surface is holding moisture.
  • Peeling or flaking old finish. A film-forming product applied over dirty or damp wood lifts in sheets. Anything new applied on top of it will lift with it.
  • A few soft, cupped or split boards among many good ones. That is a board replacement job inside a restoration, not a reason to tear out the deck.

The honest version

If you press a screwdriver into a joist, a post base or a stair stringer and it sinks in, you are not looking at a restoration — you are looking at structural and rot repair first. We check that before we quote a finish, because refinishing a deck with a failing ledger is money spent on a deck you should not be standing on.

This is the part most quotes leave vague. Restoration is a surface-and-components process. It does a great deal, and there are specific things it does not touch.

What is the deck restoration process, step by step?

  1. Inspection and written estimate. We check the ledger connection, framing, posts, footings, railings and stairs, then itemize what the restoration includes and what needs repair first. Free, and in writing.
  2. Strip and clean. Old film finishes come off with a stripper; growth and dirt come off with a cleaner and controlled-pressure washing. Nothing about this step is about blasting the wood — see our deck power washing and stripping page for why pressure is the thing that ruins decks.
  3. Brighten and neutralize. Strippers and cleaners leave the wood chemically open. A brightener resets the pH and pulls the colour back toward natural so the new finish takes evenly.
  4. Repairs. Bad boards out, new boards in, fasteners upgraded, railing posts re-secured. Doing this before finishing is what keeps a restored deck from looking patched.
  5. Dry time. The wood has to reach a moisture level a finish can bond to. In this climate, with humidity near 75% from August through October and roughly 45 inches of rain a year, that usually means waiting days, not hours.
  6. Sanding. Washing raises the grain; sanding knocks it back down. This is the step that turns a clean deck into a deck you can walk on barefoot.
  7. Finish. Stain or sealer applied to the boards, railings, caps, stairs and skirting, with end grain and cut edges sealed because that is where water gets in first.
  8. Walkthrough. We show you the repairs we made, the finish we used and when it will be due for maintenance.

Which finish should go on a restored deck?

Answer first: the more pigment a finish carries, the longer it lasts and the less wood grain you see. That trade-off is the whole decision. These are the service lives we plan around, and the horizontal numbers matter more than the vertical ones because the walking surface fails first — horizontal boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it.

Wood species matters too. Pressure-treated southern yellow pine, western red cedar, Ipe and mahogany all drink finish differently, and a product that performs on cedar can sit on the surface of Ipe and fail in a season. We match the product to the wood in front of us. Deck Restoration FAQs

Is deck restoration worth it, or should I just replace the deck?

Restoration is worth it whenever the framing is sound, because the framing is the expensive part of a deck. If the joists, beams, posts and ledger are solid and the problem is grey wood, a failed finish, algae, splinters and a handful of bad boards, restoring costs a fraction of replacing and buys years of service. Replacement makes more sense when rot has spread through the framing, when the structure cannot be brought up to current code, or when the repair list approaches half the price of a new deck. We make that call on site, in front of you, and we put it in writing.

How long does a deck restoration take?

Most residential restorations run three to five working days spread over a week or more, because the wood has to dry between steps. Cleaning and stripping happen on day one, repairs and sanding follow, and then the deck needs a stretch of dry weather before any finish goes on. Maryland humidity runs near 75 percent from August through October, which slows drying, so we schedule the finish day around the forecast rather than the calendar.

What can deck restoration not fix?

Restoration is a surface and component process, not a structural cure. It will not fix rotted joists or beams, a ledger board nailed rather than bolted to the house, missing flashing behind the ledger, posts sunk in soil without proper footings, or railings that fall short of current guard height and spacing requirements. It also will not reverse deep rot inside a board, cupping severe enough to hold water, or a board that has split through at the fasteners. Those items get repaired or replaced before any finish goes on, which is why we inspect before we quote.

How much does deck restoration cost?

There is no published Maryland-specific pricing for deck work, so treat every figure you see online as a national average. Nationally, Bob Vila puts deck refinishing at $3 to $6.50 per square foot, Fixr puts a stain job at $550 to $850 with an average near $750, and both sources put pressure washing at roughly $150 to $500. Baltimore-area pricing moves with deck size, height, access, how much wood needs replacing and which finish you choose. We quote the whole job in writing after an on-site look, at no charge.

Do I need a permit to restore a deck in Maryland?

Staining, sealing and power washing are not addressed in any local county's permit materials, so nothing in those documents says a permit is required for refinishing work. Repairs bundled into a restoration are a different question, and the answer depends on your county. Howard County and Anne Arundel County treat board-only replacement as ordinary repair with no permit. Baltimore County requires a permit for deck work on decks more than 16 inches above grade or larger than 120 square feet. Baltimore City lists no deck exemption at all. We tell you which rules apply to your address before we start.

How often will a restored deck need refinishing?

Plan on the horizontal surfaces coming due first. Water-repellent sealers generally need reapplication every 12 to 18 months, and sometimes every 6 to 12 months on a deck that takes full sun and heavy foot traffic. Semi-transparent and transparent stains typically last 12 to 18 months on the walking surface, while solid-color stain runs 1 to 3 years on horizontals and 3 to 7 years on railings and vertical faces. The simplest test is to splash water on the boards each spring: if it soaks in instead of beading, the finish is done.

Can you restore a deck that has never been sealed?

Usually yes, and it is a common call. Pressure-treated southern yellow pine that has never been sealed tends to give under 10 years of service, against 10 to 15 years with maintenance, so a never-finished deck is often grey, checked and splintering while the framing underneath is still fine. Those decks need heavier sanding and sometimes more board replacement, but they take a finish well once the surface is opened up and clean. The outcome depends on how far the checking and splitting have gone, which is what the free inspection determines.
