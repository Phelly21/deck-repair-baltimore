How Often Should You Stain a Deck in Maryland?

Different finishes, different intervals — and the walking surface always comes due long before the railings do.

Home › Blog › How Often to Stain a Deck in Maryland

It depends entirely on which finish is on the deck. Water-repellent sealers need reapplication every 12 to 18 months, and sometimes every 6 to 12 months under heavy sun and foot traffic. Semi-transparent and transparent stains run 12 to 18 months on horizontal surfaces. Solid-color stains hold 1 to 3 years on horizontal surfaces and 3 to 7 years on vertical ones.

Use those as the planning window, not the decision. The decision is made every spring with a cup of water, because two decks with the same finish applied on the same day can come due a year apart depending on sun, traffic, drainage and how much tree debris sits on them.

What are the actual re-coat intervals by finish type?

The pattern is the whole lesson: more pigment means more UV protection and a longer interval, and every finish lasts longer on a railing than on the floor. Solid-color stain on a deck's railings can still look right when the same product on the boards has been failing for two years.

Why does the deck floor always fail first?

Two reasons, and they compound each other.

  • Horizontal boards hold standing water; vertical faces shed it. A railing post is dry minutes after a storm. A deck board holds the puddle until it evaporates or soaks in, which means the finish on the floor spends far more hours actually submerged than the finish on the rails ever does.
  • The floor takes the sun and the footsteps. Overhead sun hits the walking surface squarely, and every step abrades the finish mechanically. The USDA Forest Products Laboratory documents that wood is rapidly degraded by short-wavelength ultraviolet light, with additional subsurface degradation caused by longer-wavelength violet and visible light — which is why a finish is doing protective work, not just decorative work.

And why debris makes it worse

Leaves and pine needles left on a deck block sunlight. The boards underneath stay damp long after the rest of the deck has dried, and that compounds algae and mildew growth. Come spring, the evidence is obvious — dark blotches exactly where a pile sat all winter, or a stripe of green-black along the house wall where debris collects and never blows clear. On the wooded Piedmont lots around Lutherville-Timonium and the heavy valley canopy over Ellicott City, this is the single biggest variable between a deck that makes its interval and one that does not.

The winter line is the one people underestimate. A sealed board sheds water before the cold arrives; an unsealed one has absorbed it, and every trip below freezing works that water like a wedge in the grain. Spring is when the result shows: raised grain, checking, and a surface that will not hold a finish until it is washed back and sanded.

How do you know when your deck is actually due?

Run the water-bead test in spring, after the winter and before the season of use.

  1. Pour a cup of water onto the boards in four or five places — near the house, out in the open, on the stair treads, and along the busiest walking line.
  2. Watch for a minute or two. Beads that sit on the surface mean the repellent is still working. Water that soaks in and darkens the wood means it is not.
  3. Test the rails separately. They will usually pass when the floor has failed, which is normal and is why a maintenance coat often only needs the horizontal surfaces.
  4. Look while you're down there. Grey fuzzy fibre, dark mildew blotches, raised grain and splintering all say the same thing: prep first, finish second.

Staining is not a substitute for prep. Grey, fuzzy wood is degraded surface fibre. Stain applied over it bonds to a layer that is already letting go, so the finish fails early and unevenly — often within a single season. Washing or stripping back to sound wood, sanding where needed and letting the boards dry is what makes an interval hold. That is why power washing and stripping and staining and sealing are sold and scheduled as one job here, not two.

How long should the wood itself last?

Finish intervals exist to protect a service life. For pressure-treated southern yellow pine — the most common deck lumber in this metro — the industry range runs about 10 to 15 years with maintenance, up to 15 to 25 years above ground if every field cut is resealed, and under 10 years if the wood is never sealed at all. Treat that as an industry range, not a precise figure; the real number turns on how the deck was built, how it drains and whether anyone kept a finish on it.

The field-cut detail matters. Pressure treatment protects only the wood it penetrated — every cut end, notch and drilled hole exposes untreated interior, and those are exactly where water gets in and rot starts. On the 20-to-40-year-old decks around Towson, Perry Hall and Catonsville, unsealed field cuts at joist ends and stringers are where we find the failures.

What if the finish is not the real problem?

Sometimes staining is the wrong job. If boards are cupping, feel soft underfoot, or the railing moves when you lean on it, finishing over the top hides a structural problem instead of fixing it. In that order:

  • Deck safety inspection — the fastest way to learn what you are dealing with; the deck safety checklist covers what we look at.
  • Structural and rot repair — soft joists, posts and ledgers, before anything cosmetic.
  • Deck board replacement — when boards are past the point any finish helps.
  • Full deck restoration — repair, wash, sand and finish as one sequence.

Staining a Maryland deck: frequently asked questions

How often should a deck be stained in Maryland?

It depends on the finish. Water-repellent sealers need reapplication every 12 to 18 months, and sometimes every 6 to 12 months under heavy sun and foot traffic. Semi-transparent and transparent stains run 12 to 18 months on horizontal surfaces. Solid-color stains last 1 to 3 years on horizontal surfaces and 3 to 7 years on vertical ones. Treat those as the planning window and let the water-bead test make the final call each spring.

What is the water-bead test?

Pour a cup of water onto the deck boards in several places — near the house, out in the open, on the stair treads and at the busiest walking line. If the water beads up and sits on the surface, the finish is still repelling water and can wait. If it soaks in and darkens the wood within a minute or two, the repellent has worn through and the boards are absorbing everything that lands on them. Do it in spring, after the winter has done its worst and before the deck gets used.

Why does the deck floor fade faster than the railings?

Because horizontal boards hold standing water while vertical faces shed it. A railing post sheds rain in seconds; a deck board holds a puddle until it evaporates or soaks in. Horizontal surfaces also take direct overhead sun and every footstep, which abrades the finish mechanically. That is why solid-color stain lasts 1 to 3 years on the walking surface but 3 to 7 years on the vertical faces of the same deck, finished on the same day with the same product.

Does leaf debris really damage a deck finish?

Yes. Leaves and pine needles left on a deck block sunlight, which keeps the boards underneath damp long after the rest of the deck has dried, and that compounds algae and mildew growth. The pattern is easy to spot in spring — dark blotches exactly where a pile of leaves sat all winter, or a stripe along the house wall where debris collects. Clearing debris through the autumn is the cheapest deck maintenance there is.

How long does a pressure-treated deck last in Maryland?

Industry figures for pressure-treated southern yellow pine put service life at roughly 10 to 15 years with maintenance, up to 15 to 25 years above ground if every field cut is resealed, and under 10 years if the wood is never sealed at all. Treat those as an industry range rather than a precise figure — the real number depends on how the deck was built, how it drains and whether anyone has kept a finish on it.

Can you stain over a deck that is already grey?

Not directly. Grey, fuzzy wood is degraded surface fibre, and a stain applied over it bonds to a layer that is already letting go — the finish fails early and unevenly. The fix is to strip or wash the deck back to sound wood first, sand where needed, let it dry properly, and then finish. That prep stage is the difference between a stain job that lasts its full interval and one that looks patchy by the following spring.
