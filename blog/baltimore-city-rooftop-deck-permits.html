Do You Need a Permit for a Rooftop Deck in Baltimore City?

The city's rooftop rules, the CHAP historic district process, and why a rowhome roof deck is not a backyard deck with a better view.

Home › Blog › Baltimore City Rooftop Deck Permits

Yes — always. Baltimore City has no deck size or height exemption at all: decks simply do not appear on the city's list of work exempt from permit. Rooftop decks carry an additional set of rules under the city's rooftop structures provisions, and in a CHAP historic district you need historic approval before the building permit is issued.

That is a genuinely different world from the counties. Baltimore County exempts a deck at or under 16 inches and 120 square feet; Howard County exempts anything under 25 square feet. The city exempts nothing. Here is what the rooftop rules actually say.

What does Baltimore City require for a rooftop deck?

How the 2-foot height limit is actually measured

Not from the deck surface to the roof at the nearest point. The reference is the plane midway between the lowest and highest points of the roof surface — so on a rowhome roof with any pitch or crown, the allowance at one end of the deck is different from the other. This is where rooftop decks quietly get built out of compliance: a deck framed up to sit flush with an interior door threshold can easily exceed the limit once sleepers, framing and decking stack up.

Why the setback matters more than homeowners expect

With no side or rear setback required, a rowhome roof deck can run the full width of the house between party walls — but it has to be pulled back 8 feet from the wall face on a flat roof. That single dimension is why so many Canton and Federal Hill decks sit toward the rear of the roof rather than overlooking the street, and it is worth measuring before you plan a rebuild that changes a deck's footprint.

What about CHAP historic district approval?

If the property sits in a CHAP historic district — Fells Point, Federal Hill, Upper Fells Point and Mount Vernon among them — the Commission for Historical and Architectural Preservation reviews the work, and that approval is required before the building permit. What to expect:

  • Rooftop decks are not banned, but they are often disallowed or strictly limited.
  • Review turns heavily on visibility from the street. No deck may be visible from the front of the structure.
  • A deck visible only from a rear alley may be approvable.
  • Rooftop stair penthouses — "doghouses" — are discouraged. Internal stair or hatch access is preferred.
  • Raw, unpainted pressure-treated lumber is generally not approved, which shapes both material choice and how a repair gets finished.

Where we stop and tell you to call the city. Several rooftop details circulate online that we have not been able to verify against the city's own materials — including a specific guard-height trigger for roof decks, whether horizontal cable railings are prohibited, and how long CHAP review takes. We are not going to publish numbers we cannot stand behind. Confirm those three points with Baltimore City DHCD Permits and Inspections (443-984-1809) before you design around them.

Do repairs to an existing rooftop deck need a permit?

The city's code carries general "ordinary repairs" language, but because there is no deck exemption and no published size or height threshold, a rooftop deck is the wrong place to assume. Refinishing and board-level work sit at one end of the spectrum; anything touching framing, guards, the waterproofing assembly or the roof itself sits at the other. We confirm scope with the city before that kind of work rather than after, and we tell you what we find during a deck safety inspection.

Why a rowhome rooftop deck is a different structure entirely

A backyard deck hangs off the house on a wood ledger bolted to a rim joist. A Baltimore rooftop deck usually does not: it typically spans party wall to party wall on shared masonry, bearing on structure your neighbors also depend on. That is precisely why the sealed drawings and the written notice to adjoining owners are not red tape — and why generic deck contractors get these projects wrong.

It also changes what "repair" means. On a backyard deck, the framing is the structure. On a roof deck, the roof is the structure and the deck is a passenger riding on top of a waterproofing assembly.

What actually fails on a Baltimore rooftop deck?

In order of how often we find it:

  1. Waterproofing membrane failure — number one by a distance. Blistering, alligatoring, and cracking from UV and thermal movement. Rowhome roof decks installed during the 2000s and 2010s renovation wave are now 10 to 20-plus years old, which is squarely when membranes give out.
  2. Unflashed or poorly flashed penetrations. Every post base and curb that goes through the assembly is a hole in your roof. Each one has to be flashed properly, and each one is a candidate when a ceiling starts staining.
  3. The wrong system entirely. A standard roofing membrane installed where a pedestrian deck waterproofing system belongs cracks within a couple of seasons. Roofing membranes are not built for foot traffic, furniture and point loads.
  4. Railings and guards loosening at their connections, which on a roof deck means fasteners into or near the waterproofing.

The expensive detail nobody mentions up front: once a deck sits on a roof, replacing the membrane underneath it requires removing the deck. That is why we diagnose whether a leak is the deck, a penetration, or the membrane itself before anyone starts pulling boards — and why the cheapest time to address a tired membrane is while the deck is already open for other work.

Baltimore City rooftop deck FAQs

Is there a small-deck exemption in Baltimore City?

No. Decks are absent from Baltimore City's list of work exempt from permit, which means the city has no deck size or height exemption at all — unlike Baltimore County, which exempts decks at or under 16 inches above grade and 120 square feet, or Howard County, which exempts decks under 25 square feet. In the city, assume a permit applies and confirm the scope with DHCD Permits and Inspections.

How high can a rooftop deck be in Baltimore City?

No rooftop deck may be raised more than 2 feet above the plane midway between the lowest and highest points of the roof surface. On a flat rowhome roof that leaves very little room for sleepers, framing and decking, which is why a rooftop deck that was built to sit level with a door threshold often does not comply.

What setbacks apply to a Baltimore rooftop deck?

On a flat roof, the deck must be set back 8 feet from the face of the building wall. On a gabled or mansard roof, it must sit at least 6 feet behind the ridge. No side or rear setback is required, which is why rowhome roof decks run wall to wall across the party walls but are pulled back from the street facade.

Do I have to notify my neighbors about a rooftop deck?

Yes. Adjoining property owners must be notified in writing, and evidence of that notice has to be submitted with the construction documents. On a rowhome that shares party walls with the houses on either side, this is not a formality — those neighbors share the masonry your deck bears on.

Do rooftop deck drawings need an architect or engineer?

Yes. Construction documents for a rooftop structure must be signed and sealed by an architect or engineer licensed in Maryland. That requirement exists because a Baltimore rooftop deck typically spans party wall to party wall on shared masonry rather than hanging from a wood ledger on a rim joist, so the load path runs into structure shared with the houses next door.

Does CHAP approval come before or after the building permit?

Before. In a CHAP historic district — Fells Point, Federal Hill, Upper Fells Point and Mount Vernon among them — approval is required before the building permit is issued. Rooftop decks are not banned outright, but they are often disallowed or strictly limited and are reviewed for visibility from the street. No deck may be visible from the front of the structure, while a deck visible only from a rear alley may be approvable.
