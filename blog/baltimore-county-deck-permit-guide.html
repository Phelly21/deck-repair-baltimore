Do I Need a Permit for a Deck in Baltimore County?

The county's rule is stricter than almost everyone expects — and there is no exemption for historic districts, floodplains or the Critical Area.

Home › Blog › Baltimore County Deck Permit Guide

Yes — in almost every real-world case. Baltimore County requires a building permit for any deck that is more than 16 inches above grade or larger than 120 square feet. Either one triggers the permit on its own, and there is no exemption for properties in a Historic District, on the Landmarks List, in a floodplain, or inside the Chesapeake Bay Critical Area.

That 16-inch number is what catches people out. The figure repeated on national home-improvement sites is 30 inches, so homeowners assume anything low and modest is free of paperwork. In Baltimore County a deck sitting knee-high off a walkout — or a low 12 × 12 platform — is squarely inside the requirement.

What triggers a deck permit in Baltimore County?

Two independent tests. Cross either one and you need a permit.

Why the "30-inch rule" keeps misleading Baltimore County homeowners

Thirty inches is a real number in the residential code — it is the height at which a guard becomes required around a walking surface. It is not a permit exemption, and it is not Baltimore County's threshold. Two measurements, two purposes, and the confusion costs people money when a finished deck turns out to need after-the-fact permitting. In Baltimore County, ask about 16 inches and 120 square feet.

Which deck repairs usually don't require a permit?

Permit rules are written around new structures and structural alteration. In practice, work that does not change the structure, its size, its height or its load path is generally not what the permit process is aimed at. That typically covers:

  • Refinishing work — power washing, stripping, sanding, staining and sealing. These are not addressed in any Baltimore-area county's permit materials, Baltimore County's included. That is not a published exemption, but it is not structural work either.
  • Like-for-like fastener and hardware work — corroded screws, loose connections, a rusted post base swapped for a properly rated one.
  • Cosmetic repairs — a cracked board, a splintered stair nosing, a section of lattice or privacy screening.

Board-only replacement: Baltimore County is silent. Howard County and Anne Arundel County both explicitly treat replacing deck boards as ordinary repair that needs no permit. Baltimore County's published materials do not address board-only replacement at all — not to allow it, not to prohibit it. We are telling you exactly that rather than guessing, because assuming parity across county lines is how homeowners get caught. If your project is a full resurfacing, call the county with your address and describe the scope before you begin.

Anything that touches framing — sistering or replacing joists, rebuilding a beam, resetting posts or footings, reattaching or replacing a ledger, adding stairs, changing the deck's footprint — is structural, and structural work is where permits and inspections come in. That is the honest dividing line, and it is the one we walk homeowners through before a structural and rot repair job starts.

What inspections does Baltimore County require?

Three, in sequence. Each has to be passed before the next stage of work is covered up.

  1. Footing inspection. Holes are dug and open, inspected before any concrete is placed. This is the one that trips up DIY projects — once concrete is in, it cannot be inspected.
  2. Framing inspection. The structure is framed, ledger attached, hardware installed and visible — before decking boards cover it.
  3. Completion inspection. The finished deck, including guards and stairs.

The sequence tells you what an inspector actually cares about: connections, hardware and footings — all invisible once the deck is finished. It is also why we photograph framing work as we go.

What code does Baltimore County build decks to?

Baltimore County adopted the 2021 International Residential Code, effective September 3, 2024. That matters for two reasons. First, neighboring jurisdictions are not all on the same edition — Howard County has moved on to the 2024 IRC, so a Columbia deck and a Timonium deck are being measured against different books. Second, and more practically:

The county's own deck guide predates its current code adoption. Baltimore County's homeowner deck-guide PDF was published under an earlier code edition, so the span tables and details in it are not guaranteed to match what is enforced today. Use it for orientation, then confirm current span tables and details directly with the county before you cut lumber.

Baltimore County deck permit FAQs

Does a deck under 30 inches need a permit in Baltimore County?

Usually yes. Baltimore County's trigger is 16 inches above grade, not 30 inches. A deck 20 inches off the ground needs a permit in Baltimore County even though the same deck would fall under the height threshold people quote from national articles. And height is only half the test — anything larger than 120 square feet needs a permit no matter how low it sits.

Do I need a permit to replace rotted deck boards in Baltimore County?

Baltimore County's published permit materials do not address board-only replacement one way or the other. Howard County and Anne Arundel County both explicitly treat board-only replacement as ordinary repair with no permit required, but Baltimore County is silent on it — so we do not assume the same answer applies. Call the county's permits office with your address and describe the exact scope before you assume a resurfacing job is exempt.

Do I need a permit to stain or power wash a deck in Baltimore County?

Staining, sealing and power washing are not addressed in any Baltimore-area county's permit materials, including Baltimore County's. That is not the same as a published exemption, but a finish that does not touch structure, framing, guards or stairs is not the kind of work the permit process is written around. If your project also involves replacing framing or rebuilding a railing, that structural portion is what determines whether a permit is required.

What inspections does Baltimore County require on a deck?

Baltimore County inspects decks at three points: a footing inspection before concrete is placed, a framing inspection once the structure is framed but before it is covered, and a completion inspection at the end. The footing inspection is the one homeowners miss most often, because it has to happen while the holes are still open.

Is my deck exempt because it is in a historic district or near the water?

No — it is the opposite. Baltimore County applies no exemption to properties in a Historic District, a Landmarks List area, a floodplain, or the Chesapeake Bay Critical Area. Work that might otherwise fall under a threshold still needs review in those areas, and additional approvals can apply on top of the building permit.

How deep do deck footings have to be in Baltimore County?

Baltimore County specifies footings at a 30-inch frost depth with a minimum thickness of 10 inches, poured with 3,000 psi concrete. Footings located within 3 feet of the house wall must bear at the same elevation as the house foundation, so a footing tucked close to the foundation is deeper than the standard 30 inches.

Rules change at every county line

Baltimore County's 16-inch and 120-square-foot test is one of six different answers in this metro. If your property sits over a county line, start here instead: Howard County deck permits and Columbia's village approval process, or Baltimore City rooftop deck permits and CHAP review.
