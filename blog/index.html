Deck Repair Blog

Straight Answers About Baltimore-Area Deck Repair

Permit rules that change at every county line, maintenance intervals built around Maryland's actual climate, and honest guidance on when a deck is worth saving.

Home › Blog

Most deck advice online is written for nowhere in particular. It quotes a 30-inch permit rule that Baltimore County does not use, a staining interval that ignores 45 inches of rain a year, and a repair-or-replace answer written by companies that only build decks.

These posts are written for this metro specifically. Six counties plus Baltimore City set six different permit triggers; Maryland's humidity and freezing swings shorten every finish interval on the tin; and the decks coming due right now were mostly built in the 1980s and 1990s, to rules that have since changed. Everything here is the same answer we give on the phone — including the times the honest answer is that a deck needs replacing rather than repairing.

Permits & Code

Permit rules, county by county

The single most expensive assumption homeowners make is that the rules are the same across the county line. They are not — and the differences are large enough to change what a project costs.

Do I Need a Permit for a Deck in Baltimore County?

The county's trigger is 16 inches above grade or 120 square feet — far stricter than the 30-inch figure repeated on national sites, and there is no exemption for historic districts, floodplains or the Critical Area. Covers all three required inspections and the county's own footing, lumber and hardware specs.

Baltimore City Rooftop Deck Permits & CHAP Review

Rowhome rooftop decks answer to their own chapter of the city code: a two-foot height limit above the roof plane, eight-foot setbacks, written notice to party-wall neighbours and a Maryland architect's or engineer's seal. Plus what CHAP review adds in Fells Point, Federal Hill and Mount Vernon.

Read the guide →

Howard County Deck Permits & Columbia's Village Approval

Howard County exempts only decks under 25 square feet and has moved on to the 2024 IRC, so a Columbia deck is measured against a different code book than a Timonium one. Columbia adds a second layer: recorded village covenants and an architectural committee application before any deck work begins.

Deck Care & Maintenance

Keeping a Maryland deck alive

Finish intervals printed on a can assume average conditions. Maryland is not average — roughly 45 inches of rain a year, a humidity peak from August through October, and a winter that crosses freezing again and again.

How Often Should You Stain a Deck in Maryland?

Real re-coat intervals by finish type, from 12–18 months for a water-repellent sealer to 3–7 years for solid-color stain on vertical faces. Explains why the walking surface always comes due first, how leaf debris feeds mildew, and the spring water-bead test that settles the question in two minutes.

Safety & Structure

What actually fails, and how to spot it

Deck failures happen at a short and predictable list of points. Knowing which ones to check turns a vague worry about a deck into a specific, fixable scope of work.

The Deck Safety Inspection Checklist

A walk-through of every point a deck gets judged on — ledger and flashing, joists, beams, post bases, hardware corrosion, guard height and baluster spacing, stair rise and run, and handrail graspability. Written so you can run it yourself before deciding whether to call anyone.

Why 1980s Decks Fail at the Ledger

The ledger is the most failure-prone connection on a deck, and decks from the 1980s were routinely nailed rather than bolted, left unflashed, and attached in ways the code now prohibits outright — including to brick veneer. Explains what current requirements demand and what a retrofit involves.
